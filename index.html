How the two calculators fit together

Start with the body recomposition calculator. It sets a daily calorie target from your height, weight, age, sex and activity using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, then splits that target into protein, fat and carbohydrate. Use it when you want one number to eat to each day.

Use the protein intake calculator when the calorie side is already settled and the question is only how much protein to eat and how to spread it. It returns a daily range and a per-meal figure, which is the part most people get wrong — hitting a daily total but taking most of it in one sitting.

The two agree by design: the protein figure the recomposition calculator produces sits inside the range the protein calculator returns for the same bodyweight.

What body recomposition actually means

Recomposition is losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time, rather than doing each in separate phases. It is slower on the scale than a straight cut, because the two changes partly cancel out in bodyweight while body composition shifts underneath.

It works best for people who are new to structured training, returning after a break, or carrying enough body fat to fuel muscle growth in a small deficit. Trained lifters near their genetic ceiling generally do better alternating dedicated gaining and cutting phases. The calculator says which case you are likely in based on the inputs you give it.

Because the scale moves slowly, judge progress on a three to four week view using waist measurements, how lifts are progressing and how clothes fit — not on daily weigh-ins.

Protein: the number that does the work

Protein is the input with the strongest evidence behind it. The ISSN position stand on protein and exercise places the useful range at roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day for people who train, with about 0.25 g per kg per meal as a sensible serving to trigger muscle protein synthesis.

For an 80 kg lifter that is about 128–176 g a day, and roughly 20 g in each meal as a floor. Spreading intake across three or four meals is easier to hit than two large ones, and makes the per-meal threshold straightforward.

Food values in the examples come from USDA FoodData Central, so a figure on this site can be traced to a public database rather than a rounded number from a blog.

How to use the results

Treat the output as a starting point and let two to three weeks of real data correct it. If weight and waist are both flat and lifts are progressing, the target is working. If nothing moves in either direction after three weeks, adjust calories by a small amount rather than overhauling the plan.

Weigh yourself under the same conditions each time, ideally several mornings a week, and compare weekly averages rather than single readings. Day-to-day swings from water, sodium and food volume are larger than the change you are trying to detect.

Neither calculator is medical or dietary advice, and neither accounts for medical conditions, medication or pregnancy. If any of those apply, or something here conflicts with advice you have been given, follow the advice.

Why trust these numbers

Calories start from the Mifflin-St Jeor resting energy equation published by Mifflin, St Jeor et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition 1990;51(2):241–247, multiplied by one of five standard activity factors (1.2 to 1.9). The protein range of 1.6–2.2 g per kg comes from the break-point analysis in Morton et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine 2018, which put the plateau in training-induced gains in fat-free mass at 1.62 g/kg/day with a 95% confidence interval of 1.03 to 2.20. The ISSN position stand on protein and exercise (Jäger et al., 2017) is the cross-check. Food protein figures come from USDA FoodData Central. Sources were last checked on 19 September 2026. Common questions

How do I calculate my body recomp?

Estimate maintenance calories, subtract about 10%, then set protein at 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight and fat at roughly 25% of calories, leaving carbs as the remainder. More in Body Recomposition Macro Calculator.

How long does body recomposition typically take?

There is no reliable published timeline, and anyone quoting one is guessing. What you can measure is your own trend: compare weekly average weight, waist measurement and lift performance over two to three week blocks. More in Body Recomposition Macro Calculator.

Can I recomp at 25% body fat?

Yes, and higher body fat is generally the easier starting point, because there is more stored energy available to support training while you eat slightly below maintenance. More in Body Recomposition Macro Calculator.

Is 3 months enough for body recomp?

Three months is enough to see a measurable trend, not necessarily enough to finish. Expect changes you can read on a tape measure and in your lifts before changes you can see in a mirror. More in Body Recomposition Macro Calculator.

Is 100g of protein a day enough for a woman?

It depends on body weight. At 1.6–2.2 g/kg, 100 g a day covers a woman of roughly 45 to 62 kg (100 to 137 lb) who strength trains. More in Protein Intake Calculator.

How much protein do I need by age?

This calculator does not vary the range by age: it applies 1.6–2.2 g per kg to any adult body weight you enter. More in Protein Intake Calculator.
